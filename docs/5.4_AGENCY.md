# 5.4 The Agency Gap

*What action is allowed: who may authorise it, and the world it must remain valid inside.*

Every agent, in every sense of the word, acts on behalf of someone; the question agentic tools leave unanswered is *who*, and that answer is where agency begins.

The complaints this section answers look like caution and like recklessness, and they are the same failure seen from two sides. The agent renamed a config key and warned about a migration path for consumers who do not exist; it refused to delete a dead module without a deprecation shim no one asked for. Or the reverse: told to "do whatever you need," it mutated infrastructure, rewrote a public interface, and deleted a migration, acting on a licence far broader than the one it was given. None of this is a failure of the goal, the lineage, or the structure. Each is a failure of **agency**, the action-bearing context the agent was never handed and so inferred from the conversation.

The whole-context section named agency the fourth component, beside comprehension, trajectory, and expression. The first three are *content*: what the work is, how it came to be, how it is shaped. Agency is *action*: what may be done to all of it, by whom, and within what bounds.

## Agency Model

```text
AGENCY     = AUTHORITY + GOVERNANCE
AUTHORITY  : whom the agent acts for, and what that licenses it to do
GOVERNANCE : what still constrains the action, even when the authority is valid
```

**Authority** is the standing that licenses the action: the intersection of what the principal actually has the right to decide, authorise, or delegate, and what the agent has actually been granted in this session or task. A user can wave through a change they have no standing to approve, and an agent can be handed far less, or far more, than the bare prompt implies. A travel agent who learns the client is nine years old has no valid authority, however eager the *yes*: the standing to authorise was never there. In software the same void is invisible, because the agent never sees who is on the other end.

**Governance** asks what still constrains the action once it is allowed: the external constraint surface, the operating world the action must remain valid inside, which no local *yes* can waive. A developer may own a repository and still be barred from mutating production. A platform engineer may have deploy authority and still owe the active Kubernetes context, the target namespace, the Azure subscription and resource group, the maintenance window, and the blast radius. A P.Eng may have authority to sign off on a customer build and still be bound by provincial specifications, professional standards, safety margins, and contract terms.

> Authority grants the right to act. Governance defines the world the action must remain valid inside.

Risk lives inside agency, but as one input (how much a wrong action would cost), not the controlling idea. An earlier draft made risk the whole story, under the name *stakes*, and treated it as a static regime sitting outside the context. That was too narrow: it captured how cautiously to act but not who could authorise the action or what bounds survived the authorisation, and it called the thing static when in fact it drifts. Agency subsumes the risk story and corrects both errors: it is a context component like the others, and it decays like them.

## The Entropy of Agency

> The **Entropy of Agency** is the decay that occurs when authority and governance are inferred from the conversation instead of declared as a stable layer of context.

This is a true entropy, not a setting fixed once, because agency drifts across a session. A task opens as observe-only; a few turns in, the agent is patching; a few more and it is refactoring; soon the work implies deployment, and no one updated the authorisation state. The agent accumulates conversational nudges into an implied authorisation surface, and that surface ends up too broad, too narrow, or in the wrong operating world. The drift is invisible because each nudge is small and locally reasonable, and because a model optimised for agreement reads the most recent nudge as the standing licence.

It has the same shape and cause as the other three entropies: a part of the context that should be declared and held is instead re-inferred at the speed of the prompt. Governance is the slow layer (the operating world changes rarely but must be known almost every time the agent acts), and forcing it through the prompt channel is the pace-layer failure the whole-context section described: a slow truth pushed through a fast channel. Authority is the faster layer, drifting turn by turn. Both decay when neither is written down.

### Overdeclared authority

The user says "do whatever you need," and the agent takes it literally. But the user almost always means something narrower: do whatever is needed *inside this repository*, without touching production, deleting migrations, breaking a public API, mutating infrastructure, violating a contract, or bypassing review. Overdeclared authority lets the agent read a local licence as a global one. It acts boldly where its authority was never that broad, and because the bold action satisfies the literal request and passes its tests, nothing in the loop reports the breach until the cost has landed.

### Underdeclared authority

The user gives vague or timid authorisation: "look at this," "clean this up," "maybe fix it." The agent cannot tell whether deletion, renaming, interface changes, stale-test removal, migration edits, or deployment are inside its remit. The result is timidity (incomplete work, unnecessary questions, dead structures preserved, shims nobody needs) and, most characteristically, *fusion*: old code kept while new code is added beside it, because adding is the lower-authority act and removal asserts a licence the agent was never sure it had. This is the trajectory section's fusion from the agency side: there, the agent could not see the old state was wrong; here, it can, and still lacks the authority to say so.

### Missing governance

Authority can be valid and the work can still fail, because the external constraint surface is absent: missing, stale, or pointing at the wrong world. The agent knows what the user wants and what it may do, and still acts in the wrong place.

- cleared to edit Kubernetes manifests, but no knowledge of whether the target is dev, staging, or prod;
- a valid change applied while the active kube context points at the wrong cluster;
- a modification to Azure infrastructure made without the subscription or resource-group boundary in view;
- a customer build changed without the governing provincial specification in scope;
- compatibility code deleted without knowing a public-API commitment exists.

None of these is a risk miscalculation; the agent may have weighed risk perfectly. They are governance failures: the authorised action was correct in one operating world and invalid in the one it ran in. These facts (dev versus staging versus prod, provider, subscription, resource group, cluster, namespace, mutability, blast radius) rarely change, yet without a place to declare them the user restates them at prompt-speed every session, or omits them and hopes the agent infers the right world. It frequently does not.

### The risk story, corrected

An agent told nothing about where it stands falls back on the base rate of its training distribution, dominated by production systems with real users, because production is what the world writes code and documentation about. Absent a declared world, it assumes the modal one and over-protects: migration anxiety (caution for consumers that do not exist), ceremony inflation (branch-and-PR process for a one-week-old solo repo), stakeholder hallucination ("your users might expect…" about a project with no users), and stale-test preservation (old tests bent around rather than deleted, the verification surface treated as load-bearing when it encodes the very architecture the change means to replace). The same modal-world assumption runs the other way under overdeclared authority, emboldening rather than restraining. This is the least settled failure mode in the paper, named as an observation that warrants investigation rather than an established result: by construction the harm is a tax on velocity and a slow distortion of architecture, not a clean attributable break.

### Prior art and intervention

Declared rather than assumed authority sits between two literatures. In security the idea is foundational: Saltzer and Schroeder (1975) named *least privilege* and *fail-safe defaults*, granting the minimum authority an action requires and denying by default; the traditions that followed all rest on authority being explicitly conferred and bounded, never inferred. The second lineage calibrates caution to the situation: Boehm and McConnell's project *mode* (ceremony scaling with size and stakes), Bell, Litt and Marwick's *imagined audience* and *context collapse* (an agent with no declared world is the limit case), Tversky and Kahneman's *anchoring* toward the modal world, and Vaughan's *normalisation of deviance* in the other direction. Each assumed a situated human who knew the bounds because they were in the project. The agent is situated nowhere, and acquires its authority only by guessing.

The intervention is to stop inferring agency and declare it: a small, explicit layer read before the agent acts, naming both halves.

```yaml
agency:
  authority:
    user_role: project_owner
    agent_delegation: patch_and_refactor
    destructive_actions: approval_required
  governance:
    environment: staging
    cloud: azure
    resource_group: example-rg
    cluster: example-aks
    namespace: example-ns
    mutable: limited
    governing_rules:
      - deployment parity with prod
      - signed customer specification
      - public API compatibility
    forbidden:
      - mutate prod
      - delete persistent volumes
      - bypass required approval
```

The shape is illustrative, not prescriptive: the keys lean on one domain's vocabulary, and a regulated profession or a physical-world build would name entirely different bounds. The point is not the schema. It is that the theory implies a system primitive: a place where authority and governance are stated once and held, so the slow truth stops being re-guessed every prompt. With it, the agent can ask the question that is stronger than "be careful," because it separates authority from the constraints that survive it:

> *Whom am I acting for, what does that license me to do, and what governance boundaries must the action still obey?*

## Agency is not only about code

Nothing in this section is specific to software. Authority and governance bound action wherever it has consequences outside the immediate artifact. The platform engineer's cluster and namespace, the P.Eng's provincial specification and safety margin, the operator's maintenance window, the analyst's data-handling jurisdiction: each is an operating world that survives any local approval, and each is a thing a capable agent must be told because it cannot read it off the work in front of it. As agents move from editing code to acting on infrastructure, organisations, regulated professions, and the physical world, the agency gap stops being a matter of caution and becomes the difference between an action valid where it lands and one that is not.

---

The model did not get worse. The standing it has to act from got harder to read. Authority and governance were never declared before, because the person acting already held them; the agent holds neither and is told neither, so it infers a licence from the conversation and a world from its training. The interventions here are not about making the model timid. They are about declaring whom it acts for, what that permits, and the world the action must stay valid inside, so a local *yes* stops being read as a global one and the right change stops landing in the wrong world. Precision about authority, set before the agent acts, is cheaper than the breach discovered after, and the difference compounds across every action taken under a licence no one set.
