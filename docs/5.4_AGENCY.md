# VII. The Agency Gap

*What action is allowed: who may authorise it, and the world it must remain valid inside.*

The complaints this section answers look like caution and like recklessness, and they are the same failure seen from two sides. The agent renamed a config key and warned about a migration path for consumers who do not exist; it refused to delete a dead module without a deprecation shim no one asked for. Or the reverse: told to "do whatever you need," it mutated infrastructure, rewrote a public interface, and deleted a migration, acting on a licence far broader than the one it was actually given. None of this is a failure of the goal, the lineage, or the structure. Each is a failure of **agency**, the action-bearing context of the work, which the agent was never handed and so inferred from the conversation.

The whole-context section named agency the fourth component, set beside comprehension, trajectory, and expression. The first three are *content*: what the work is, how it came to be, how it is shaped. Agency is *action*: what may be done to all of it, by whom, within what bounds.

```text
AGENCY     = AUTHORITY + GOVERNANCE
AUTHORITY  : whom the agent acts for, and what that licenses it to do
GOVERNANCE : what still constrains the action, even when the authority is valid
```

**Authority** asks whom the agent acts for, and what that relationship licenses. Every agent, in every sense of the word, acts on behalf of someone; the question agentic tools leave unanswered is *who*, and that answer fixes the licence. It is the intersection of what the user actually has the right to decide, authorise, or delegate, and what the agent has actually been granted in this session or task. A user can wave through a change they have no standing to approve, and an agent can be handed far less, or far more, than the bare prompt implies. A travel agent who learns the client is nine years old has no valid authority, however eager the *yes*: the standing to authorise was never there. In software the same void is invisible, because the agent never sees who is on the other end.

**Governance** asks what still constrains the action once it is allowed. It is the external constraint surface: the operating world the action must remain valid inside, which no local *yes* can waive. A developer may own a repository and still be barred from mutating production. A platform engineer may have deploy authority and still owe the active Kubernetes context, the target namespace, the Azure subscription and resource group, the maintenance window, and the blast radius. A P.Eng may have the authority to sign off on a customer build and still be bound by provincial specifications, professional standards, safety margins, and contract terms.

> Authority grants the right to act. Governance defines the world the action must remain valid inside.

Risk lives inside agency, but as one input (how much a wrong action would cost), not the controlling idea. An earlier draft of this paper made risk the whole story, under the name *stakes*, and treated it as a static regime sitting outside the context. That was too narrow in two ways. It captured how cautiously to act but not who could authorise the action or what bounds survived the authorisation; and it called the thing static when in fact it drifts. Agency subsumes the risk story and corrects both errors: it is a context component like the others, and it decays like the others.

## The Entropy of Agency

> The **Entropy of Agency** is the decay that occurs when authority and governance are inferred from the conversation instead of declared as a stable layer of context.

This is a true entropy, not a setting fixed once, because agency drifts across a session. A task opens as observe-only; a few turns in, the agent is patching; a few more and it is refactoring; soon the work implies deployment, and at no point did anyone update the authorisation state. The agent accumulates conversational nudges into an implied authorisation surface, and that surface ends up too broad, too narrow, or located in the wrong operating world. The drift is invisible because each nudge is small and locally reasonable, and because a model optimised for agreement reads the most recent nudge as the standing licence.

It has the same shape as the other three entropies and the same cause: a part of the context that should be declared and held is instead re-inferred at the speed of the prompt. Governance is the slow layer (the operating world changes rarely but must be known almost every time the agent acts) and forcing it through the prompt channel is the pace-layer failure the whole-context section described: a slow truth pushed through a fast channel. Authority is the faster layer, drifting turn by turn. Both decay when neither is written down.

### Overdeclared authority

The user says "do whatever you need," and the agent takes it literally. But the user almost always means something narrower: do whatever is needed *inside this repository*, without touching production, deleting migrations, breaking a public API, mutating infrastructure, violating a contract, or bypassing review. Overdeclared authority lets the agent read a local licence as a global one. It acts boldly in a space where its authority was never that broad, and because the bold action satisfies the literal request and passes its tests, nothing in the loop reports that the licence was exceeded until the cost has already landed.

### Underdeclared authority

The user gives vague or timid authorisation: "look at this," "clean this up," "maybe fix it." The agent cannot tell whether deletion, renaming, interface changes, stale-test removal, migration edits, or deployment changes are inside its remit. The result is timidity (incomplete work, unnecessary questions, dead structures preserved, compatibility shims nobody needs) and, most characteristically, *fusion*: old code kept while new code is added beside it, because adding is the lower-authority act and removal asserts a licence the agent was never sure it had. This is the trajectory section's fusion seen from the agency side: there, the agent could not see that the old state was wrong; here, it can, and still lacks the authority to say so.

### Missing governance

Authority can be valid and the work can still fail, because the external constraint surface is absent from the context: missing, stale, or pointing at the wrong world. The agent knows what the user wants and what it has been allowed to do, and still acts in the wrong place.

- cleared to edit Kubernetes manifests, but no knowledge of whether the target is dev, staging, or prod;
- a valid change applied while the active kube context points at the wrong cluster;
- a modification to Azure infrastructure made without the subscription or resource-group boundary in view;
- a customer build changed without the governing provincial specification in scope;
- compatibility code deleted without knowing a public-API commitment exists.

None of these is a risk miscalculation; the agent may have weighed risk perfectly. They are governance failures: the authorised action was correct in one operating world and invalid in the one it actually ran in. Authority answers whether the action may be authorised. Governance answers what the authorised action must still obey.

The infrastructure case is the sharpest, and it generalises. Dev, staging, and production contexts change slowly, but must be known almost every time the agent acts; so must the cloud provider, subscription, resource group, cluster, namespace, mutability, blast radius, and deployment authority. These facts rarely change, yet without a place to declare them the user is forced to restate them at prompt-speed every session, or far more often, to omit them and hope the agent infers the right world. It frequently does not.

### The risk story, corrected

The failures an earlier draft filed under *stakes* are all agency failures: valid-or-invalid authority, read against a misjudged operating world. An agent told nothing about where it stands falls back on the base rate of its training distribution, dominated by production systems with real users, because production is what the world writes code and documentation about. Absent a declared world, it assumes the modal one and over-protects: migration anxiety (caution for consumers that do not exist), ceremony inflation (branch-and-PR process for a one-week-old solo repo), stakeholder hallucination ("your users might expect…" about a project with no users), and stale-test preservation (old tests bent around rather than deleted, because the agent treats a verification surface as load-bearing when it encodes the very architecture the change means to replace). The same modal-world assumption runs the other way under overdeclared authority, where it emboldens rather than restrains. This is the least settled failure mode in the paper, named as an observation that warrants investigation rather than an established result: by construction the harm is a tax on velocity and a slow distortion of architecture, not a clean attributable break. What is defensible now is the diagnosis: an agent given no authority assumes one, and the one it assumes is wrong in a consistent, measurable direction.

### Prior art and intervention

That a system's authority should be *declared* rather than *assumed* sits at the meeting point of two literatures.

The first is security, where the idea is foundational. **Saltzer and Schroeder (1975)** named *least privilege* and *fail-safe defaults*: grant the minimum authority an action requires, and deny by default rather than allow *(Saltzer, J.H. & Schroeder, M.D., 1975)*. The traditions that followed (access control, capability systems, separation of duties) all rest on the premise that authority is explicitly conferred and bounded, never inferred from context. Agentic tooling has, for the most part, abandoned that premise: the agent's authority is whatever the conversation seems to imply.

The second is the literature on calibrating caution to the situation, which the risk story inherits. **Boehm (1981)** built project *mode* into COCOMO: ceremony scales by whether a project is organic, semi-detached, or embedded *(Boehm, B., 1981)*; **McConnell (2004)** made the craft version, process weight as a function of size and stakes *(McConnell, S., 2004)*. The social sciences supplied the missing force, the audience: **Bell (1984)** named *audience design* *(Bell, A., 1984)*, and **Litt (2012)** and **Marwick & boyd (2011)** the *imagined audience* and *context collapse*, where invisible real audiences flatten into a single conservative default; an agent with no declared world is the limit case *(Litt, E., 2012; Marwick, A. & boyd, d., 2011)*. **Tversky & Kahneman (1974)** named the *anchoring* that drags the guess toward the modal world *(Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D., 1974)*; **Vaughan (1996)** documented the opposite drift, *normalisation of deviance* *(Vaughan, D., 1996)*. Each literature assumed a situated human who knew the bounds because they were in the project. The agent is situated nowhere, and acquires its authority only by guessing.

The intervention is to stop inferring agency and declare it: a small, explicit layer read before the agent acts, naming both halves.

```yaml
agency:
  authority:
    user_role: project_owner
    agent_delegation: patch_and_refactor
    destructive_actions: approval_required
  governance:
    environment: staging
    cloud: azure
    resource_group: example-rg
    cluster: example-aks
    namespace: example-ns
    mutable: limited
    governing_rules:
      - deployment parity with prod
      - signed customer specification
      - public API compatibility
    forbidden:
      - mutate prod
      - delete persistent volumes
      - bypass required approval
```

The shape is illustrative, not prescriptive. A real declaration needs mixins fitted to the scope of the work, and the keys above lean on the vocabulary of one domain; a version for a regulated profession or a physical-world build would name entirely different bounds. The point is not the schema. It is that the theory implies a system primitive: a place where authority and governance are stated once and held, so the slow truth stops being re-guessed every prompt. With it, the agent can ask the question that is stronger than "be careful," because it separates authority from the constraints that survive it:

> Whom am I acting for, what does that license me to do, and what governance boundaries must the action still obey?

## Agency is not only about code

Nothing in this section is specific to software. Authority and governance bound action wherever action has consequences outside the immediate artifact. The platform engineer's cluster and namespace, the P.Eng's provincial specification and safety margin, the operator's maintenance window, the analyst's data-handling jurisdiction: each is an operating world that survives any local approval, and each is a thing a capable agent must be told because it cannot read it off the work in front of it. As agents move from editing code to acting on infrastructure, organisations, regulated professions, and the physical world, the agency gap stops being a matter of caution and becomes the difference between an action that is valid where it lands and one that is not.

---

The model did not get worse. The world it is allowed to act in got harder to see. Authority and governance were never written down before, because the person acting was situated in the project and carried both without thinking. That person is no longer the one acting. The intervention here is not about making the agent braver or more timid in general. It is about replacing an inferred agency with a declared one (authority stated, governance pinned) so that the agent acts boldly where it has been granted licence, stops where it has not, and stays valid in the world it is actually running in. Precision about agency, declared before the agent acts, is cheaper than discovering after the fact that the action was valid in the wrong world.

## Experiments

**The modal-world anchor.** Run a battery of identical refactor and breaking-change tasks under three framings: (a) a declared personal greenfield with no users; (b) a declared production system with consumers; (c) no agency declared. Score each on a caution profile: migration warnings, deprecation shims, tests preserved versus deleted, ceremony suggested, breaking changes refused. The two-part hypothesis: that (a) and (b) differ sharply and appropriately, and that the undeclared (c) clusters near the production end rather than the midpoint. A confirmed (c)-with-(b) result is the core evidence that agency, when undeclared, is assumed toward the training distribution's modal world.

**Declared agency as action control.** Across the same battery, supply a structured agency declaration (authority and governance both) and measure whether the action profile moves to track it: the caution profile, the agent's declared definition of done, and the rate at which it attempts actions outside the stated bounds. The hypothesis is that a coarse declaration captures most of the available correction, telling the intervention's designers how minimal the artifact can be.

**Overdeclared authority against a hidden boundary.** Give the agent a broad licence ("do whatever you need") on a repository seeded with a governance boundary it is not told about: a production manifest, a public API with external consumers, a migration with a downstream dependency. Measure how often the broad licence drives it across the hidden boundary, and whether an explicit governance declaration (the boundary stated, not just risk implied) catches the crossing that the licence alone invites. This is the direct test of authority read as global when it was only local.

**Missing governance in the wrong world.** Place the agent in an environment whose operating world differs from the modal one (an active kube context pointing at a non-default cluster, a non-production subscription, a staging namespace marked limited-mutability) and issue a valid, permitted change. Measure how often it acts as though the world were the modal one (mutating as if prod, or refusing as if prod) rather than reading the declared governance. The prediction is that without a governance layer the agent's action is uncorrelated with the actual world, and with one it tracks it.
